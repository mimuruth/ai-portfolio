## Engineering Lab 4 — Hybrid retrieval and fusion from scratch

### The mental model

Picture your corpus as a **library**. **BM25** is the card catalogue: it finds
books whose *words* match your query — great for exact terms like an error code,
blind to synonyms. **Vector search** is a knowledgeable librarian who understands
*meaning* — it finds "run on a schedule" when you asked about a "timer trigger,"
but can miss an exact rare token. **Hybrid retrieval** consults both and merges
their recommendations; the **reranker** is a specialist who reads your query and
each shortlisted book *together* and puts the best one on top.

### Mathematical formulations

**Reciprocal Rank Fusion.** Each retriever returns a ranked list. A document $$d$$
that appears at rank $$\text{rank}_i(d)$$ in list $$i$$ scores

$$\operatorname{RRF}(d)=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{k+\text{rank}_i(d)},$$

with a small constant $$k$$ (commonly $$60$$). Because the score depends only on
**rank**, not on each retriever's raw score, RRF needs no calibration between
BM25's term scores and cosine similarities — a document ranked highly by *either*
retriever floats up; ranked highly by *both*, higher still.

**Retrieval quality.** With $$R$$ the set of truly relevant chunks for a query and
$$K$$ retrieved:

$$\text{Precision@}K=\frac{|R\cap\text{retrieved}_K|}{K},\qquad \text{Recall@}K=\frac{|R\cap\text{retrieved}_K|}{|R|}.$$

**Mean Reciprocal Rank** rewards putting the first relevant hit early, averaged
over $$Q$$ queries:

$$\text{MRR}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\frac{1}{\text{rank of first relevant hit for }q}.$$

### Bare-metal implementation — RRF over two rankers

Pure Python, no vector database, to make the fusion unmistakable:

```python
def rrf(rankings, k=60):
    """rankings: list of ranked lists of doc ids (best first). Returns fused order."""
    scores = {}
    for ranked in rankings:
        for rank, doc in enumerate(ranked, start=1):
            scores[doc] = scores.get(doc, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)

bm25   = ["d3", "d1", "d7", "d2"]   # lexical ranking
vector = ["d1", "d4", "d3", "d9"]   # semantic ranking
print(rrf([bm25, vector]))
```

> **EXAMPLE — output.**
> ```
> ['d1', 'd3', 'd7', 'd4', 'd2', 'd9']
> ```
> `d1` wins: it is ranked highly by *both* retrievers (2nd lexical, 1st semantic).
> `d3` is next (1st lexical, 3rd semantic). Neither retriever alone produced this
> order — that is the value of fusion.

### Chunking, quantified

Chunk size trades **precision** against **recall of complete facts**. Too small and
a passage loses the context that makes it answerable; too large and each hit drags
in irrelevant text that dilutes the prompt and raises token cost. `prod-rag` uses
**500–800 token** chunks with **~100 token** overlap; the overlap $$o$$ guarantees
any fact shorter than $$o$$ that straddles a boundary survives intact in at least
one chunk.

> **Production note — "lost in the middle."** Models use facts placed near the top
> or bottom of a long context more reliably than facts buried in the middle. This
> is *why* you retrieve a small, reranked handful instead of stuffing the window:
> shorter, better-ordered context beats more context. It also keeps input tokens —
> and therefore the measured **$0.00025/request** — low.
